01

Position

AI is loud. Kūhaku is the quiet around it.

Kūhaku is an operating system built around one screen. You say what you want, an AI does it, and the answer comes back in large, light type. Around that screen sit a few apps drawn the same way: Mado for the web, Tegami for mail, Miru for files, Tsukuru for making things.

A machine that can do almost anything tends to show almost everything. Kūhaku's design has one job: to keep that power at the edge of attention until the moment it is needed.

The idea has a name, calm technology. Researchers at Xerox PARC described it in 1995: a calm technology moves easily from the edge of our attention to the centre, and back. Kūhaku applies it to a computer that an agent operates.

Quiet and useful sometimes pull apart. One sentence settles it: function decides whether a thing exists; design decides how.

This document is the whole design: what it borrows, its ten laws, how a mark is drawn, every icon, every control, how to compose a screen, a window, a page and a sentence, and the arguments that settled it all.

02

The seal

One red mark on an empty sheet.

空白, kūhaku, is blank space: the part of a sheet that is left on purpose. An ink painting is black on paper, and its only colour is the artist's seal, pressed once in vermilion. The seal says that someone is here.

Kūhaku keeps that rule. A view is paper, ink and one red mark. Red says here: what is yours, what is open, where you are, or where you are needed.

03

Origins

Where it comes from, and where the likeness ends.

Kūhaku is not Japanese design and it is not Scandinavian design. It was made in neither place, and dressing it up as either would be costume. It borrows one idea each from five places. For each, this is what it takes and where the borrowing stops.

Japanese ink painting and flower arranging

In both arts the empty part of the work is composed as carefully as the full part, and a painting is finished with one red seal. Kūhaku takes exactly these two things: space that is left on purpose, and one red per mark.

It does not take the brush. An ink line is made by a moving hand; it swells, fades and is never the same twice. Kūhaku's line is ruled, one weight, identical every time. Anything that imitates a brush stroke or paper grain stays out.

The Bauhaus and Swiss graphic design

These schools built everything from the line, the square and the circle, set it on a grid, and let a plain typeface order the page by size and weight alone. This is the part that fits best. It is the skeleton of every Kūhaku icon and layout.

Their colour does not fit. The Bauhaus put red, yellow and blue side by side. Kūhaku has one colour.

German product design of the 1960s

The radios, shavers and calculators of that school were grey or white all over, with one coloured button on the thing you press. Kūhaku uses red the same way: as a signal, never as decoration.

It does not copy the machines. Knobs, dials and sliders drawn on a flat screen imitate hardware that is not there. Kūhaku's controls are words, squares and bars.

Scandinavian design

Here the look mostly does not fit, and it is better to say so. Scandinavian furniture and houses are warm: wood, wool, soft curves. Kūhaku's line is hard and its marks are cold. What it takes is the attitude behind those objects: a thing should look obvious, and the work that made it obvious should not show. It also takes two small things: paper that is warm white instead of pure white, and soft corners on the sheets that marks sit on.

Calm technology

The idea that a computer should keep most of what it knows at the edge of your attention. The other four are how Kūhaku looks. This one is why.

Put together: a Japanese idea of space, a Swiss skeleton, a German use of colour and a Scandinavian attitude, in the service of calm. A new design that cannot say which of these it comes from probably does not belong.

04

Laws

Ten laws. Each one can be tested.

  1. One red per mark

    A mark, a row or a panel carries at most one red element.

    Test: cover the red. Exactly one meaning disappears.

  2. Red means here

    What is yours, what is open, where you are, where you are needed. Nothing else is coloured.

    Test: finish “this is red because” in five words.

  3. State changes in place

    A state recolours or moves a stroke. It never adds a symbol, a colour or a column.

    Test: lay every state over the others. The outline is one.

  4. One pen

    One weight, square ends, mitred corners. A corner is sharp, or it is a whole circle.

    Test: the drawing needs no linecap, no linejoin and no radius.

  5. Five strokes

    A mark takes one to five strokes of the pen. Most take two or three.

    Test: count. At six, the idea has not been found yet.

  6. Straight first

    Lines at 0°, 45° and 90°, then the square. A curve is a true circle, kept for things that are round or soft: a lens, a clock, water.

    Test: could a ruler and a compass draw it?

  7. Space is a material

    The margin is drawn as carefully as the mark. Remove before you add.

    Test: take each part away in turn. If it still reads, the part stays away.

  8. Quiet until needed

    At rest everything is grey and at the edge. Attention is asked for once, in place, and given back.

    Test: nothing moves or changes colour unless something changed.

  9. Day and night are equals

    Every mark and every screen is drawn for both and checked in both.

    Test: real size, both themes, at rest and on.

  10. It still works

    Quiet never costs function. Function decides whether a thing exists; design decides how.

    Test: used once, it is remembered.

5.3Surface and line

Surfaces are soft: a sheet has a radius of 18, a hairline edge and one shadow. Marks are hard: no radius on a corner. The paper may be soft; the pen is not.

5.4Why these

None of the materials is a matter of taste alone. Each was chosen for a reason that can be checked.

  • InterIt was drawn for screens, so it stays clear at 12 pixels. It is neutral in the Swiss way: it carries the words and adds no voice of its own. One file holds every weight, so order can come from weight and size alone, and its figures line up in columns. It is free and open source, like Kūhaku. Helvetica, its obvious ancestor, was drawn for print and has to be licensed.
  • Light weightsA light letter puts less ink on the screen, and less ink is quieter. It needs size and a sharp screen, so nothing is set light below 12.5.
  • Warm paperPure white on a screen is a lamp. A white with a little warmth in it reads as a surface, and a surface is easier to look at for a day.
  • Ink, not blackOne step off pure black. The contrast on paper is still 17 to 1, without the hardness of black on a lit screen.
  • VermilionThe red of a seal, pulled toward orange. It is the colour the eye finds first, which is exactly why there is only one of it.
  • A lighter red at nightOn a dark sheet the day red sinks to a contrast of 4.1. The night red is lighter and brings it up to 6.4.
  • A grid of 24It divides by 2, 3, 4, 6, 8 and 12, so halves, thirds and quarters all land on whole units. Many icon sets use it, so drawings can be compared.
  • A line of one pixelThe thinnest line a screen draws cleanly. Thinner blurs. Thicker begins to shout.
  • Square ends, 45 degreesA square end lands on the pixel grid; a round end at one pixel is always a blur. After level and upright, 45° is the one angle that pixels draw evenly.
  • Soft sheetsA sheet floats above the page. Its soft corner sets it apart from the hard marks it carries, with no border needed.

6.2The pen

One pen draws everything. It sets down, travels straight, and lifts.

These are the defaults of the medium. An SVG line with no stroke-linecap and no stroke-linejoin already has a square end and a mitred corner. The round look of most icon sets comes from overriding that; Kūhaku leaves the line as it is.

  • WeightOne pixel. In units, 24 divided by the size, and never under 1.
  • CornerThe runs of a corner are one stroke, so they mitre.

6.4The hot stroke

A mark has one stroke that is the idea. At rest it is grey like the others. When the thing is open, it turns red.

  • Which strokeThe part that acts: the cursor, the lid, the roof, the shackle.

  • OrderDrawn last, on top.
  • GlyphsTools such as close, back and find have no hot stroke. They act; they are never open.

Method

Six questions, asked in order.

Every mark in the library went through the same six questions. They work for an icon, a control or a whole page. The fourth and the sixth do most of the work: keep cutting until it breaks, then put the last piece back.

  1. Symbol

    What sign do people already know for this? Draw it whole. Then ask whether it is the right symbol or only the usual one.

  2. Simplification

    Draw it again with the pen: straight first, sharp corners, on the cell. Leave out everything that is decoration.

  3. Function

    Does it still say what the thing is or what it does? Show it to someone with no label beside it.

  4. Further

    Take one more part away and ask the third question again. Repeat until the answer is no.

  5. Simple and smart

    Is there an idea in it, or is it only a thinner version of everyone's icon? A smart mark uses what Kūhaku already says: a dot is live, a lid is a title bar.

  6. Too far?

    If the last cut lost the function, the action or the character, step back one. The step before the break is the mark.

Then choose the hot stroke, look at it at real size by day and by night, and use it for a day. Three marks, start to finish:

10

Practice

How to design anything this way.

Icons are the smallest case. A screen, a window, a website and a sentence are made by the same thinking. Kūhaku is less a look than a way of deciding: ask what can be left out, leave it out on purpose, and make what remains exact.

10.1Five questions before you draw

  1. What is the one thing?

    Every screen is for one thing. Say it in a sentence. If the sentence needs “and”, it is two screens.

  2. What can wait?

    List everything else. Each item either waits until it is asked for, or goes. Nothing stays because there was room.

  3. Where is the red?

    In one place: where the person is, or where they are needed. If nothing qualifies, there is no red, and that is fine.

  4. What does the space do?

    Empty space is placed. It should lead the eye to the one thing. If it is only what was left over, the layout is not finished.

  5. Will it survive a whole day?

    Calm is felt over hours. Anything that moves, glows or counts will be seen a thousand times. Design for the thousandth.

10.2A screen

  • 1 · The top rowThe only bar on the screen. The red line on the left opens everything; status on the right stays grey.
  • 2 · The one thingIn large, light type. On this screen it is an answer. Under it, one grey line for what is worth knowing and no more.
  • 3 · Where you writeThe caret is the second and last red: it is where you are.
  • 4 · The spaceMost of the screen. It is what makes the answer the only thing you read.
  • One edgeText and marks hang from one left edge. Centre a thing only when it is alone on the screen.
  • Three sizesAt most three sizes of type on one screen, and two weights. More sizes mean the order has not been decided.
  • Space, then a lineSeparate things with space first and a hairline second. Never with a box, a fill or a shadow.
  • About sevenIf more than about seven things are in view at rest, some of them should be waiting.
  • Nine to oneRoughly nine parts paper to one part ink, and a trace of red. Squint: if the screen looks grey, it holds too much.

10.3A window

An app in Kūhaku is one window with one bar. This is Miru, the files app.

  • 1 · The lineThe same red line as the menu, in the same corner, then the name and one grey number. It is how you know the window is Kūhaku's.
  • 2 · ActionsWords, in the bar, in grey. Only the few that apply to what is selected; the rest appear when they can be used.
  • 3 · PlacesA list of words. Where you are is ink; the others are grey.
  • 4 · The stageThe content gets everything else. No second toolbar, no status bar, no frame around it.
  • 5 · Two controlsMinimise and close, at 12. There is no third.

A window has no title bar apart from this one bar, no menu bar and no icons standing in for words. A glyph is used only for something pressed all day, such as back or close. If a window needs a second bar, it is doing two jobs.

10.4A website

A page about Kūhaku, or made for it, follows the same laws. This document is built by them and can be read as the example.

  • One idea a screenEach screenful says one thing, and its heading says it as a plain sentence. “The computer you talk to.”
  • One columnText in one column about 62 characters wide, hanging from one edge. A narrow rail beside it for labels, if needed.
  • Show the thingPictures are the real interface at real size, inside a hairline. No device frames, no stock photographs, no drawings of people.
  • One actionA page asks for one thing. That is its one ink block; every other link is a grey word.
  • One redThe red line of the wordmark or the menu. A button is never red, and neither is a headline.
  • Space as the dividerSections are set apart by a lot of space and one hairline. No bands of colour, no cards, no gradients.
  • Quiet behaviourNothing pops up, slides in, plays by itself or follows the pointer. The page is finished when it has loaded.
  • Both themesDay and night, each drawn with care. The night page is its own design and never the day page inverted.

10.6Motion

Rise and settle

A thing arrives from just below where it will rest, in about a third of a second, and slows as it lands. It never bounces, spins or overshoots. It leaves the way it came, faster.

The pulse

A slow pulse means that work is going on, and it means only that. There are no spinners and no bars creeping across the screen.

  • Only on changeNothing moves unless something changed. Motion is how a change is noticed, so it is never decoration.
  • One curveEvery movement uses the same easing, cubic-bezier(.2, .7, .2, 1), between a quarter and a third of a second.
  • Asked to stopIf the person has asked for less motion, things appear in place.

10.7Empty, waiting, wrong

  • EmptyAn empty screen is the normal state and needs no excuse. No drawing, no tip of the day. At most one grey line saying what could be here.
  • WaitingThe pulse, and after a few seconds a sentence saying what is being done. Always a way to stop.
  • WrongWords in ink, at the place where it went wrong. Red only if the person has to act.
  • NoticeIt arrives at the edge, once, and leaves by itself. If it needs you it stays, and that is the only time it is red.

10.8The last check

Before anything ships, each of these gets a yes.

  1. Is there one thing, and is it the first thing seen?
  2. Is there at most one red in each row and panel, and can it finish “this is red because”?
  3. Has everything that could wait been made to wait?
  4. Does every state change in place?
  5. Was the night version drawn, and looked at?
  6. Does it read at its real size, on a real screen?
  7. Are the words short, plain and exact?
  8. Does it still do its job? Quiet never costs function.
11

Cases

The laws were not written first. Each was learned from a case.

These are the arguments that shaped Kūhaku, with what was tried, what was thrown away and why. They are kept because the reasoning is more useful to the next designer than the result.

The battery

The battery began as a bar: 18 pixels by 6, filling with ink. It was one of the best things in the top row. Then it had to show charging. First came a small bolt beside the bar. It was not wrong, only dull, and it made the sign one column wider, so everything next to it moved. Then the bolt went and the fill turned green. The footprint was right again, but a second colour had arrived in a system that had one.

Both attempts answered the wrong question. Charging does not need you. It needs nothing at all, so it earns no colour and no symbol. What a person does want to know is where the charge is going, because Kūhaku stops at 80% to protect the battery and fills to the top only before a trip.

So the bar shows the target. While the cable is in, a one pixel tick stands where the charge will stop, and the fill grows toward it. When they meet, the bar looks as it does at rest, because there is nothing left to say. This case gave Kūhaku two laws: state changes in place, and red means here.

Five times real size.

The drag bar

Volume and brightness were set by dragging a small red knob along a thin red line. It was a picture of a hardware fader, the knob was nine pixels wide, and it was red for no reason. It never looked right.

Kūhaku already had a way to show an amount: the battery. The level is that same bar, made pressable. Press anywhere and the fill goes there. There is no knob, because the end of the fill is the handle, and the number beside it says exactly how much. One shape now means “an amount” everywhere.

The square

The on and off switch was a pill with a dot that slid across and turned red. Five switches on meant five red pills in one panel, and red had stopped meaning anything.

The replacement is the smallest thing that can hold two states: a square, empty or filled. Then the question was how to choose one of three. The first answer was words with a red line under the chosen one. It looked good and it was wrong. Red says where you are, and the value of a setting is not a place. So a choice is the switch repeated: a word and its square for each option, one square filled. The red line stayed where it is true, under the tab you are on.

A settings panel is now paper and ink, with one red mark. Because every setting ends in a square, the Settings mark is drawn the same way: a name and its square.

Work

An account can be Home or Work. Home was a house and it was right at once. Work was a briefcase: three parts, rounded, and an object few people carry any more. It was the usual symbol, and nobody had asked whether it was the right one.

Home is drawn as the place you live. So Work is the place you work: a desk, a top and two legs. This case gave the method its first question. Start from the symbol people know, then ask if it is the right symbol or only the familiar one.

How much red

The menu line is red. A dot beside it turned red while apps were open, and each open app also turned one stroke of its mark red. With three apps open, the top row held five reds, and the one that mattered was lost among them.

The top row keeps red in two places: the menu line, which is the seal, and the open stroke of a mark. The dot beside the line is ink. It still says that something is open; the strokes say what.

Round or square

For a while every new icon came out rounder than the last: soft corners, round line ends, shapes borrowed from the icon sets everyone uses. The first five marks had none of that, and they were the ones that looked like Kūhaku. So the pen was fixed: square ends, sharp corners, one weight.

That rule could have gone too far in the other direction. A square magnifying glass is harder to read than a round one, and nothing is gained. A lens, a clock, the power sign, the cradle of a microphone and the waves of calm water keep their true circles. The cloud is built in steps, because a cloud has no true shape to keep. Kūhaku is sharp where a thing is made and round where a thing is round.

Smaller decisions

  • Four sizes12, 16, 18 and 40. The line weight follows from the size. The quick row stays at 18 because its five marks are the front door.
  • One drawingEach meaning has one drawing, used everywhere. Close is not redrawn for each app.
  • 45 degreesThe roof of Home and the fold of Tegami are both at 45°, and the lids of Mado and Tegami overhang by the same unit.
  • KanseiOne square pulse, in a single stroke of straight lines.
  • A dotA dot is an app in the top row and in the Apps mark. Elsewhere it means live.
  • ButtonsA word, in three strengths. The strongest is a word on an ink block, one per screen. No button is red, not even a dangerous one.
  • The composerOne upright red line: the caret, where you write. The menu is the same line lying down. The dot it used to carry is gone.
  • One redEvery red comes from the same token. No red is written by hand.
